\section{Design Process or Methodology Overview}
The design followed the goals and limits from Chapter~3, using a ``test before you build'' process with four rules: (i) build a strong, simple baseline before any complex model; (ii) measure the best result a design could ever reach before spending training time on it; (iii) fix the train/test split by contract once, use it everywhere, and never tune on the test set; and (iv) test each step on its own and then the whole system from start to finish. The result is a three-step system with a risk layer on top.

The system works in steps (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}). The contract text is cut into windows. The presence step decides which of the 41 categories the contract contains. The span step finds the exact clause text for each category found. The risk layer adds the \emph{category's} High\slash Medium\slash Low level, and a text-generation step adds a plain-English sentence. The final output is a report sorted by risk. (The Clausify app runs every step; Section~\ref{sec:clausify}.) Section~\ref{sec:summ} shows that the last step does not really summarize the clause in front of it: it picks one of 41 sentences written in advance. So the system is better described as ``sorting into categories and showing a template'' than as summarizing. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} names the steps and the models behind them. Figure~\ref{fig:dataflow} at the end of this chapter shows the same steps with the actual data passed between them, which is useful when reading the implementation.

One idea runs through this whole chapter: \textbf{build a strong, cheap baseline first, and measure the best possible result of a design before spending training time on it}. Both habits changed the design in real ways, as the next sections show.

\section{Preliminary Design or Design (Model) Specification}
For each step we considered other designs and, where possible, tested them before choosing one. For finding which clauses are present, we compared three designs (a simple word-based model that reads the whole contract, a ``search first, then classify'' transformer, and a transformer that reads the contract in sliding windows) and three ways of combining the simple model with the transformer. We also considered sparse-attention models and rejected them for the reasons in Section~\ref{sec:lit_long}; we measured their cost later (Section~\ref{sec:lfbench}). The design chosen for each step is described below.

\subsection{Stage 2A: Presence Classification}

\subsubsection{A strong baseline first: full-document TF--IDF}
Simple bag-of-words models have no limit on input length, so a TF--IDF model can read the \emph{entire} contract, which no windowed transformer can do. We built a TF--IDF vocabulary (20{,}000 features, single words and word pairs, sublinear TF, $\text{min\_df}=2$) from the 408 training contracts and trained one balanced logistic-regression classifier per category. Legal writing is very repetitive (``\emph{This Agreement shall be governed by the laws of the State of \ldots}''), so the words alone carry a lot of information. This baseline sets the score every neural model has to beat.

\subsubsection{Measuring the retrieval ceiling, and dropping retrieval}
\label{sec:ceiling}
The obvious way to give a long contract to a transformer is to search first: cut the text into 2{,}000-character windows that move forward 1{,}500 characters at a time (the windows are drawn in Figure~\ref{fig:windows}), score each window by its TF--IDF similarity to the category, and give the model only the best window or windows. The search uses only information that is available when the app runs (the category name or its CUAD question, never the answer), so no answer information leaks in.

Before training anything, we measured the \emph{search hit-rate}: how often does the chosen window really contain the clause? Figure~\ref{fig:ceiling} shows how this works and why the result is a hard limit. Whatever this number is, no model after the search can do better, because a clause in a window the search never picks can never be found. The best search we tried (using the category name, keeping the top 3 windows) reached only \textbf{64.0\%} (Table~\ref{tab:retrieval}). Surprisingly, the CUAD question text did worse than the plain category name, because the questions share so much standard wording that they all look almost the same. A recall limit of 0.64 is well below the baseline's recall of 0.81 (and its micro-F1 of 0.775), so a search-first transformer would lose before it was even trained. This measurement took seconds and saved us a wasted training run.

This number needs one note. We measured the limit of \emph{word-based} search only: every search in Table~\ref{tab:retrieval} compares windows by shared words, so a clause written without the words of its category name cannot be found even when it is clearly there. A meaning-based search (one that turns both the question and each window into vectors) matches meaning instead of shared words and would probably raise the 64.0\% figure, possibly above the baseline. We did not test one. So the fair conclusion is not ``search does not work'', but that \emph{word-based} search sets a limit that is too low to build on. That was enough to move us to the window design in Section~\ref{sec:mil}. Whether meaning-based search could raise the limit enough to compete is an open question that we list as future work.

\subsubsection{Sliding-window max-pooling (multiple-instance learning)}
\label{sec:mil}
To remove this limit, we trained a \emph{window-level} classifier instead. Given a pair (category question, window), it predicts whether that window contains a clause of that category. Figure~\ref{fig:windows} shows how the windows are made. A 2{,}000-character window that moves forward 1{,}500 characters at a time leaves a 500-character overlap between neighbouring windows. This overlap makes sure no clause can fall between two windows and be lost: the typical (median) clause is 196 characters long (Section~\ref{sec:eda}), much shorter than the overlap, so every clause fits completely inside at least one window.

The training labels come from the marked character positions: any window that contains a marked clause is positive, and for each (contract, category) pair we also take two random negative windows. Using the marked positions this way is normal supervised learning, not leakage: the labels only decide the training targets, they are never part of the model's input, and nothing from the answers is used when the app runs. From the 408 training contracts this gives 53{,}662 window examples (39.2\% positive).

When the app runs, the model scores \emph{every} window of the contract, and the answer for the whole contract is simply the highest window score. This is the multiple-instance learning rule, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:mil}. The document is a \emph{bag} of windows; the bag is positive if any window is positive; and taking the $\max$ does exactly that. Because the windows cover the whole document with overlap, every clause can be reached, so the search limit disappears. The cost is precision, and Figure~\ref{fig:mil} also shows why: the maximum is taken over every window, so a single wrong window anywhere is enough to mark the whole contract as positive. A typical contract has 22 windows (the median; the mean is 35), so the chance that some window passes the 0.5 threshold by mistake grows with length: long contracts are more likely to give a false alarm than short ones, whatever they actually contain.

\subsubsection{The conjunctive ensemble}
When we compared the two models category by category, they turned out to \emph{complement} each other: the transformer does better on categories where meaning matters (for example \emph{No-Solicit of Employees} and \emph{Liquidated Damages}), while the simple model does better on categories with clear keywords. We combine them with rules that have \textbf{no settings to tune} (AND, the $\min$ of the two scores; OR, the $\max$; and AVG, the average). We chose such rules on purpose, so that nothing can be fitted to the test set. Figure~\ref{fig:ensemble_rule} shows the decision. The AND rule, which reports a clause only when both models agree, removes many false alarms: for a wrong detection to survive, both models must make the same mistake on the same contract and category, and their mistakes are mostly independent. This wins back the precision that taking the maximum loses.

\subsection{Stage 2B: Span Extraction}
\label{sec:spanmethod}
For this step the transformer has no replacement: a bag-of-words model cannot point to positions in the text. We fine-tune \texttt{DistilBertForQuestionAnswering}: the input is (CUAD question, window text), and the model predicts where the answer starts and ends. The character positions in \texttt{CUAD\_v1.json} are turned into token positions using the tokenizer's offset mapping.

Two different window sizes are used in this project, and it is worth being clear about them, because mixing them up makes the design look inconsistent. The 2{,}000-character window of Section~\ref{sec:mil} is a \emph{presence} window: it covers the document in order, because at that point we do not know where anything is. The window used here is a \emph{focus} window: it is 1{,}200 characters long, and instead of covering the document in order it is placed around the marked answer, so the answer starts about 150 characters in. Figure~\ref{fig:focus} draws the two at the same scale. The smaller, centred window is needed for a simple technical reason: it makes sure the answer is not cut off by the model's 320-token limit. With a 2{,}000-character window, an answer near the end could be cut off, and the model would be trained on an example whose answer is missing. Also note that this step has no length limit at all: it only searches \emph{inside} a window that the presence step has already marked as relevant.

\subsection{Stage 1: Plain-Language Summarization}
\label{sec:summmethod}
CUAD gives us clauses but no plain-English versions to learn from, so we wrote our own: \textbf{41 template sentences}, one carefully written one-sentence summary for each category, written from the weaker party's side. For \emph{Anti-Assignment}, for example, the template is ``You cannot transfer this contract to someone else without permission.'' Each training clause is paired with its category's template, which gives 11{,}156 training pairs, and FLAN-T5-small is fine-tuned on the prompt ``\texttt{summarize in plain English: <clause>}''.

We test the summarizer with ROUGE-L \emph{and} by comparing, on the same clauses, the fine-tuned model with the original model before fine-tuning. We use both tests on purpose. With only 41 different target sentences, a model can get a high ROUGE score just by recognising the category and copying its template word for word, without really summarizing anything. Chapter~5 shows that this is exactly what happens, and we report it that way.
\subsection{Category-Level Risk Prioritization}
\label{sec:risk}
First, a note on the name, because it limits what we can claim. The system does not read a clause and judge how dangerous \emph{that} clause is. It finds which of the 41 categories a contract contains and gives each category a fixed level. These are different tasks, and mixing them up would greatly overstate the system: our layer says \emph{``contracts with a non-compete deserve attention''}, never \emph{``this non-compete is unusually harsh''}. So throughout this report we call it \textbf{category-level risk prioritization}, not risk classification.

\subsubsection{Assignment criteria}
\label{sec:riskcriteria}
We did not set the levels by gut feeling alone. We state the rules we used so that a reader, or a reviewing lawyer, can disagree with a specific level for a specific reason. Each category was scored on five points, from the weaker party's side:

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Money at risk.} Can this clause cost money, and is there a limit? Having no limit is the strongest single sign of High risk.
  \item \textbf{Cannot be undone.} If the clause takes effect, can the situation be reversed? Losing ownership of intellectual property or giving a permanent licence cannot be undone later; a notice period can.
  \item \textbf{Limits on future freedom.} Does the clause limit what the party can do \emph{after} the contract, or outside it, such as who it may work for, sell to or hire?
  \item \textbf{One-sided.} Does the duty fall on only one side? A shared duty of confidentiality is less dangerous than a one-way indemnity with the same wording.
  \item \textbf{How long it lasts.} Does the duty continue after the contract ends?
\end{enumerate}

The rule we used: \textbf{High} if the category has no money limit (point~1), cannot be undone (point~2), or limits freedom after the contract (point~3). \textbf{Low} if the category only gives information (it records a fact such as a date, a name or the governing law, or gives a benefit) and scores on none of the five points. \textbf{Medium} otherwise: a real duty, but limited and reversible. This is why \emph{Cap on Liability} is Medium and not Low even though it sounds protective (it limits how much you can recover, which may not cover a real loss), and why \emph{Governing Law} is Low even though it matters in a court case (it is a fact about the contract, not a one-sided duty on the signer).

The eight High categories fall into three groups, each based on a different point: money you could owe without limit (\emph{Uncapped Liability}, \emph{Liquidated Damages}, \emph{Minimum Commitment}, \emph{Most Favored Nation}); something you cannot get back (\emph{IP Ownership Assignment}, \emph{Irrevocable/Perpetual License}); and your freedom to earn later (\emph{Non-Compete}, \emph{Exclusivity}). The full table with reasons is in Appendix~\ref{app:taxonomy}, and Figure~\ref{fig:riskdist} shows how the categories are spread across the levels.

\subsubsection{Who is the ``weaker party''?}
\label{sec:weaker}
This phrase is important in the risk table, and it can mean different things, so we explain how we used it. The weaker party is not always the same role: in a distribution contract it may be the distributor, in a licence the licensee, and in a franchise the franchisee. In a balanced contract between equals there may be no weaker party at all.

We defined it as \textbf{the party with less power over how the contract was written}, which in CUAD is usually the party that did not write it. Where this was unclear from the category alone, we scored from the side of \textbf{the party who carries the duty}: \emph{Non-Compete} is scored from the side that is restricted, \emph{Audit Rights} from the side that is audited, and \emph{License Grant} from the side that receives the licence. This is a rule of thumb, and it has a known weakness: a clause that is High-risk for one side often protects the other side, so the same badge is not equally useful to a licensor and a licensee. A system that judged each clause separately could work out the side from the clause text; ours cannot, so it assumes the user is the side with less power. The app states this assumption in the notice shown above every analysis.

\subsubsection{Why a lookup table rather than a learned score}
The risk table is a simple lookup, not a model output, and that is on purpose: it can be checked line by line, its reasoning is written down for each category, and it cannot change silently between versions. The cost of this choice is the limit named at the start of this section: it cannot tell a \$10{,}000 liability limit from a \$10{,}000{,}000 one. Section~\ref{sec:riskeval} measures a second cost we did not expect: because the levels are fixed per category, the system's mistakes depend entirely on which \emph{categories} it finds well, and the High-risk categories turn out to be among the ones it finds worst.


\subsubsection{Provenance and validation status of the taxonomy}
\label{sec:taxvalid}
This risk table is the main thing this project adds to CUAD, and it is also the part with the weakest evidence behind it, so we state exactly where it comes from.

\textbf{Who wrote it.} All 41 levels were set by the three of us. We are computer science students, not lawyers. We set the levels by thinking about the effect on the weaker party (can this clause cost you unlimited money, take away something you cannot get back, or limit what you can do later?) and compared them with the CUAD category descriptions. But they rest only on our own judgement. No legal professional has reviewed them.

\textbf{Why this matters more here.} Every other part of this project is measured against answers marked by experts. This part is not measured against anything. It also affects the user most directly: the level decides what Clausify tells a signer to worry about first. So a mistake here is not just a lower score in a table; it is a warning that never appears. A category we marked Medium that a lawyer would mark High is a silent failure of the app.

\textbf{The check that is needed, and where it stands.} The basic check is a review by someone with legal training: give them the 41 categories with our level and reason, ask whether they agree with each one, and report the number of disagreements \emph{whatever it turns out to be}. We have prepared a review sheet for this (it is in the repository as \texttt{review/risk\_taxonomy\_review\_sheet.csv}, with one row per category and columns for the reviewer's own level and comments), and it takes a reviewer about half an hour.

One reviewer would give a basic check; \emph{two or three reviewing separately} would give a stronger one, and the difference is worth explaining. Simple percentage agreement is a poor measure for this table because the levels are uneven: 22 of the 41 categories are Medium, so a reviewer who answered ``Medium'' every time would agree 54\% of the time while adding no information at all. Agreement corrected for chance (Cohen's $\kappa$ for two reviewers, Fleiss' $\kappa$ for three or more) removes this problem, and that is what we would report. The repository includes \texttt{review/agreement.py}, which reads the completed sheets and prints each reviewer's agreement, both kappa values and the list of categories in dispute. This way the analysis is fixed in advance and cannot be shaped by the results.

\emph{At the time of writing this review has not been done, and no number from it is reported anywhere in this project.} Until it is done, the risk table should be read as a documented and checkable, but clearly unchecked, design. Its strength is that every level is written down with a reason and can be argued with line by line, not that any qualified person has agreed with it yet.

\subsection{Leakage Discipline}
\label{sec:leakage}
With such a small dataset, data leakage is the most dangerous way to fool ourselves, so we followed one clear rule throughout: \emph{the answers may be used to create training targets, but never to shape what a model reads or what is chosen when the app runs}. In practice: the split is by contract; searches use only the category or its question; the marked answer positions label training windows but are never used to build inputs or choose windows when the app runs; and the ensemble rules have no settings, so the test set cannot tune them.

One result of this is visible in Figure~\ref{fig:focus} and must be stated clearly, because it is the one place where the rule seems to be broken. The focus window is placed using the marked answer position, which is part of the answer. In training this is normal supervised learning: it decides which text becomes a training example. In the real system there is no marked answer, and the span step reads the window chosen by the presence step. The \emph{separate} span test in Section~\ref{sec:span} is the exception, and we say so plainly: it builds each test example with the same centred focus window, so it measures extraction \emph{when the position of the answer is already known}. That makes it a best-case score, not a score the real system can reach. The realistic number is the start-to-finish test in Section~\ref{sec:e2e}, which never uses the marked answer position.

\section{Data Collection}
\label{sec:datasource}
All experiments use the official CUAD release from The Atticus Project, downloaded from the Hugging Face hub (\texttt{theatticusproject/cuad}). One practical finding is worth noting: the default \texttt{load\_dataset()} option gives only the 511 original contract PDFs, which cannot be used for training. The marked data is actually stored in two files in the repository, and each one has a different job in our system:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item \texttt{master\_clauses.csv}: a wide table with 83 columns and one row per contract, which we used to explore the data and build labels;
  \item \texttt{CUAD\_v1.json}: the SQuAD-format file with the full contract text and the character positions of every answer, which we used to train the span model and to read whole contracts.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Data Cleaning}
The CUAD files were prepared by experts, so cleaning was about fixing format problems rather than wrong values. Four problems had to be handled:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Empty cells.} Clause-text cells that are empty (\texttt{NaN}) are treated as an empty list, which means the category is absent.
  \item \textbf{Badly formed lists.} Each clause-text cell holds a Python list written as text. Cells that cannot be read as a list are kept as one clause instead of being thrown away, and blank entries are removed from every list.
  \item \textbf{Inconsistent column names.} The column name \texttt{Notice Period To Terminate Renewal- Answer} contains an extra space that breaks matching by name, so columns were paired by their position instead (Section~\ref{sec:datasource}).
  \item \textbf{Empty answers in the JSON file.} Answers that are empty after removing spaces are ignored when building the span-training examples and the answer lists.
\end{itemize}
No unusual values were removed: very long contracts are real and were kept, because handling length is exactly what the method must do.

\subsection{Data Transformation}
The CSV is wide: 41 categories $\times$ (a clause-text column plus an answer column), with a file-name column at the front. Turning it into a long table with one row per (contract, category) required two decisions that were not obvious at first:

\textbf{Pair columns by position, not by name.} The column name \texttt{Notice Period To Terminate Renewal- Answer} contains an extra space, which silently breaks any matching between a category column and its answer column by name. Pairing by column position (clause text in odd columns, answer in even columns) is not affected by such typos.

\textbf{Decide presence from the clause-text list, not the Yes/No answer.} Each clause-text cell holds a Python list written as text, either \texttt{'[]'} or \texttt{"['clause', ...]"}. We say a category is \emph{present} when this list is not empty. This rule works the same way for the eight categories (dates, party names) whose answer column holds a value instead of Yes/No, and it also gives us the clause text directly.

This gives $510 \times 41 = 20{,}910$ labelled (contract, category) rows, of which 32.1\% are positive.

Three more steps prepare the data for the models. \textbf{Windows:} every contract is cut into 2{,}000-character windows that move forward 1{,}500 characters at a time, so neighbouring windows overlap by 500 characters, which is more than a typical clause, and no clause falls between two windows. \textbf{Position conversion:} for the span model, CUAD's character positions are turned into start and end token positions using the tokenizer's offset mapping, inside a 1{,}200-character focus window around each answer so that it fits in 320 tokens. \textbf{Turning text into numbers:} for the baseline, each whole contract becomes a TF--IDF vector of single words and word pairs, with sublinear term frequency. Category labels are kept as simple present/absent values; no other scaling of numbers is needed beyond what TF--IDF does.

\subsection{Data Integration}
The two CUAD files complement each other. \texttt{master\_clauses.csv} gives one row per contract with the clause lists we used to explore the data and build labels, while \texttt{CUAD\_v1.json} gives the full contract text, the 41 category questions and the character positions of every marked clause. All the modelling uses the JSON file: presence labels, answers and contract texts all come from it, matched by contract title and by the category name taken from each question ID. The CSV is used to explore the data and to double-check how often each category appears. The fixed split by contract is computed once over the 510 contract titles and applied in the same way to every table made from the data.

\subsection{Data Reduction}
We reduced the data only to keep training possible on a laptop and to balance the classes, never to throw away information when the app runs:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Vocabulary limit for the baseline.} The TF--IDF vocabulary keeps only the 20{,}000 most common single words and word pairs that appear in at least two contracts.
  \item \textbf{Fewer negative examples.} For window-level training, every positive window is kept but only two negative windows are taken for each (contract, category) pair, giving 53{,}662 window examples with 39.2\% positives.
  \item \textbf{Training on a sample.} The presence model was trained on a balanced sample of 24{,}000 windows, the span model on 8{,}000 of 11{,}156 examples and the summarizer on 4{,}000 of 11{,}156 pairs. Section~\ref{sec:budget} tests whether training the presence model on a sample hurt it.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Summary of Preprocessed Data}
\label{sec:eda}
Four features of the data shaped every design decision that followed:

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Very uneven categories.} How often a category appears ranges from \emph{Document Name} at 100\% and \emph{Parties} at 99.8\% down to \emph{Source Code Escrow} at 2.5\% and \emph{Price Restrictions} at 2.9\%. The middle (median) category appears in about 23\% of contracts.
  \item \textbf{The rarest categories have almost no examples.} \emph{Source Code Escrow} appears in only 13 contracts in the whole dataset. No model can learn such categories reliably, and their individual scores are mostly noise. We keep this warning in mind throughout the results chapter.
  \item \textbf{Contracts are long.} The median is 33{,}143 characters (about 5{,}000 words) and the maximum is 338{,}211 characters. The median is about 16$\times$ what a transformer can read at once (about 2{,}000 characters), and the longest is about 170$\times$. This is the central modelling problem of the whole project.
  \item \textbf{Clauses are short.} The median marked clause is 196 characters (90th percentile 587, 99th percentile 1{,}330). So a clause easily fits inside one window; the hard part is finding it in a very long document.
\end{enumerate}

It is important to say clearly that the 510 contracts are the whole dataset. Marking them reportedly cost about \$2M of lawyers' time, so more cannot easily be added. This has three effects: we must start from pre-trained models; over-fitting is the main danger, so all our conclusions rest on test-set numbers; and the train/test split must be made by contract.

\subsubsection{The 80/20 Train/Test Split}
As our supervisor asked, we split the dataset \textbf{80/20 by contract}: each whole contract goes to one side or the other, so no clause from a training contract can appear in the test set. The split uses a fixed random seed (42) and \emph{all three models use exactly the same split}, so ``test'' always means the same 102 unseen contracts. Table~\ref{tab:split} summarizes the split; the almost equal share of positives on both sides shows it is balanced. We saved the two sides as \texttt{data/train\_80.csv} and \texttt{data/test\_20.csv} so the split itself can be repeated.

\subsection{Validation Protocol, and Where the Hyperparameters Came From}
\label{sec:valprotocol}
A reader may fairly ask how the settings in Table~\ref{tab:hyper} were chosen. If any of them had been chosen by trying several options and keeping the one that scored best on the 20\% test set, every number in Chapter~5 would be too optimistic and the test set would no longer be truly unseen. This section answers that question directly, including one place where we did not do as well as we should have.

\subsubsection{What was tuned, and what was not}
\textbf{Nothing in Table~\ref{tab:hyper} was tuned.} Every setting is either a standard default for the model or a value fixed by reasoning before training, and no setting was ever compared with another on the test set. In detail:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Learning rates, batch sizes, epochs} ($2\times10^{-5}$, $3\times10^{-5}$, $3\times10^{-4}$; 16/16/8; 2 epochs) are the standard fine-tuning defaults for DistilBERT and T5 models. We ran each setting once and did no search. This protects the test set, but it also means we have no evidence these are good values, only that they are common ones.
  \item \textbf{Window of 2{,}000 characters, step of 1{,}500} comes from a measurement on the \emph{training} data, not from tuning: the 500-character overlap must be longer than a typical clause (196 characters) so that no clause falls between windows (Section~\ref{sec:mil}). Any window and step that meet this rule would work; we did not look for the best pair.
  \item \textbf{Two negative windows per contract and category} is a choice to balance the classes, keeping about 39\% positives instead of letting almost all windows be negative. It was not tuned.
  \item \textbf{Taking the maximum window score} was chosen because it \emph{is} the multiple-instance rule, not because it beat other options; we never tried other options. Section~\ref{sec:pooling} fixes this gap afterwards.
  \item \textbf{The 0.5 threshold} is simply the default boundary for a two-class model. It was never chosen, checked or changed. Section~\ref{sec:threshold} shows afterwards that it is a poor choice for the window-based transformer.
\end{itemize}

In short, this project avoided tuning on the test set by not tuning at all. That is a real guarantee that the test scores are fair, but it is also a limit: the transformer may lose to the baseline partly because nobody looked for better settings for it. We note both sides.

\subsubsection{One thing we got wrong}
The first exploratory run of the summarizer in the notebook, whose loss curve was shown in earlier drafts of this report, used 300 clauses \emph{taken from the test split} to monitor training. That is the wrong data to monitor with, and we should not have done it.

Two things limit the damage, and we do not use them as excuses. First, that run kept no checkpoints (\texttt{save\_strategy="no"}) and had no early stopping, so the numbers it printed could not have been used to choose a model; nothing about the model depended on them. Second, the summarizer used for all results was trained again by \texttt{build\_artifacts.py} with \emph{no} evaluation set at all, and that model, not the notebook's, produced every summarizer result in Chapter~5. So no reported score comes from a model that was chosen using test data.

What remained was that the ``validation'' loss printed by that run (0.096 $\to$ 0.075) was really a loss on test clauses, so it should be ignored. The October 2026 re-training rebuilt the summarizer exactly as it is used, with no evaluation set, and Figure~\ref{fig:sumloss} now shows that run's training loss, so no test data appears in it. We keep this mistake in the report because it is a useful lesson: with a small dataset and three models, it is easy to grab ``the other split'' when a validation set is needed. The way to catch it is to write down, for each model, exactly what was kept aside, which is what Table~\ref{tab:valsets} now does.
\subsection{Optimization Schedule}
Table~\ref{tab:hyper} gives the highest learning rate of each model, but that alone does not describe a training run; the schedule that lowers it over time does. All three models use the same optimizer setup: AdamW with a \textbf{linear decay} schedule, \textbf{no warm-up}, and gradient clipping at 1.0. Figure~\ref{fig:lrsched} plots the three schedules. The presence and span settings were read from the \texttt{training\_args.bin} file saved next to each model, not from our notes, so the plot shows what actually ran.

Two of these choices need a reason. Skipping warm-up is usually risky, but these are short fine-tuning runs (1{,}000--3{,}000 steps) that start from pre-trained weights with small learning rates. The usual reason for warm-up, keeping the first updates of a randomly started model stable, hardly applies here, and the smooth loss curves in Figures~\ref{fig:presloss} and~\ref{fig:sumloss} show no early problems. Gradient clipping at 1.0 was kept on throughout, which matters more than it may seem on the MPS backend: it stops a single bad batch from causing the kind of breakdown we saw with DeBERTa-v3 (Section~\ref{sec:engineering}).

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{lr_schedule.png}
\caption{Learning-rate schedules}
\label{fig:lrsched}
\end{figure}

We should be open about one limitation and how far we could fix it. A record of the gradient size at every step is the natural partner of these curves, and it is the one check that shows directly that training stayed healthy, instead of guessing it from the loss. We did not keep it for the three original runs: the temporary checkpoint folders were deleted after training, so only the final models and the loss values below remain from them.

We did keep it for the full-data presence run in Section~\ref{sec:budget}, which uses exactly the same optimizer setup, and it shows something the loss curves do not. Across the 63 logged points, the gradient size ranges from 0.55 to 11.97 with a median of 4.66, and \textbf{98.4\% of logged steps are above the clipping limit of 1.0}. Nothing broke (there are no NaN values, no huge spikes, and the loss falls smoothly throughout), so the runs were healthy in the way that matters. But clipping here is not the rare safety net the name suggests. It is active on almost every step, which means the update size is being cut back all the time, so the learning rate of $2\times10^{-5}$ overstates the real step size. We mention this because it slightly weakens the warm-up argument above: part of what kept the early updates stable was almost certainly the clipping, not only the small learning rate. Readers should not take ``no warm-up was fine'' as general advice based only on our smooth loss curves. Re-running the span and summarizer training with the same logging would close this gap in about half an hour.
\subsection{Training the Presence Classifier}
\label{sec:prestrain}
The TF--IDF + logistic-regression baseline trains in under a minute. On the 20\% test set it reaches micro-F1 0.775, weighted-F1 0.777, macro-F1 0.572 over all 41 categories, and 0.666 over the 34 categories that have at least ten test positives. The difference between micro and macro scores comes from the rare categories with very few examples, not from a weakness of the model.

The window-level DistilBERT was trained on a balanced sample of 24{,}000 window examples (keeping 39.2\% positives) so that training stays manageable on a laptop. In the October 2026 re-training it took 49.8 minutes. The training loss fell smoothly from 0.57 after the first 100 steps to 0.26 at the end, and the validation loss went from 0.275 after the first epoch to 0.254 after the second (Figure~\ref{fig:presloss}). The validation loss stayed close to the training loss, so we saw no sign of over-fitting.

\subsection{Training the Span Extractor}
The question-answering model was trained on 8{,}000 focus-window examples for two epochs. In SQuAD-style systems it is very easy to get the character-to-token conversion wrong without noticing, and this small check catches that.
\subsection{Training the Summarizer}
\label{sec:sumtrain}
FLAN-T5-small was fine-tuned on 4{,}000 clause--template pairs on the CPU: about 8 minutes originally, and 12.7 minutes in the October 2026 re-training (which ran while the laptop was busy with other jobs). The training loss dropped quickly, from 1.17 at step 100 to 0.08 by the end of the second epoch (Figure~\ref{fig:sumloss}). There is no validation curve because the model used in the app is trained with no evaluation set (Table~\ref{tab:valsets}). The re-trained model scores ROUGE-L 0.775 against the templates on the 150 test clauses, the same as the original. Looking back, this fast drop was the first sign of the template-copying behaviour we analyse in Chapter~5: with only 41 different targets, the task is simply easy to learn.

\subsection{Engineering Notes: Training on Consumer Hardware}
\label{sec:engineering}
A few practical lessons may save time for anyone repeating this work on Apple-Silicon computers:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{DeBERTa-v3 is unstable on MPS.} Our first-choice model produced NaN (broken) gradients on the MPS backend and was far too slow on the CPU (about 89 seconds per step). We switched to DistilBERT; the method stays the same, and on a CUDA machine the model name can simply be changed back.
  \item \textbf{MPS memory is not freed inside one program.} After two transformer trainings in the same session, the summarizer ran out of MPS memory even after we deleted objects and cleared the cache. What actually works is restarting the kernel or training the (small) summarizer on the CPU, which is what we did.
  \item \textbf{Library changes.} \texttt{transformers} 5.x renamed \texttt{Trainer(tokenizer=\dots)} to \texttt{processing\_class=\dots}. We also turned off mixed precision (\texttt{fp16=bf16=False}) and converted the loss logits to fp32 to avoid data-type problems on MPS.
  \item \textbf{Repeatability.} Everything needed (the split in \texttt{artifacts/split.json}, the baseline in \texttt{artifacts/baseline.pkl}, and the three trained models) is saved to disk, and a separate test notebook recreates every number in Chapter~5 from those files alone.
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Measured: What Sparse Attention Would Have Cost}
\label{sec:lfbench}
Section~\ref{sec:lit_long} argued against sparse attention partly because of cost, and a cost argument is weak if the cost is never measured. The laptop used for this project is exactly the machine in question, so measuring it only cost the time to run it, and we did.

We built Longformer-base with its published size (12 layers, 768 hidden units, 4{,}098 position embeddings, attention window 512, 148.7M parameters). We timed one forward pass at 4{,}096 tokens and one full training step (forward pass, backward pass and AdamW update), and compared them with the DistilBERT setup this project actually used. The weights are random, which is fine here, because time and memory depend on the size of the tensors, not on the values in them. Each setup ran in its own program, so the peak memory reported belongs to that setup alone. The machine is an Apple M4 with 16\,GB of shared memory, which the operating system also uses. Table~\ref{tab:lfbench} shows the results.

\textbf{First, this measurement corrects us.} The original wording of Section~\ref{sec:lit_long} said that fine-tuning a Longformer at 4{,}096 tokens was ``far beyond a single laptop's memory budget''. That is not what happens. At batch size 1 it fits: 14.71\,GB on a 16\,GB machine. That is very close to the limit, leaving less than 1.5\,GB for the operating system, but the model, its gradients and its optimizer state all fit in memory and the step finishes. We had claimed it was impossible, it turns out to be a tight fit, and our claim was wrong.

\textbf{The memory claim is true one step further.} At batch size 2 the memory needed rises to 20.75\,GB, more than the machine has, and the effect shows in the timing: the step takes 2{,}170 seconds instead of the roughly 516 that doubling the batch should take. That is 8.4 times slower for twice the work, which is the sign of the computer swapping memory to disk instead of computing. So the honest claim is not ``it does not fit'' but ``it fits only at batch size 1, and any batch size a real training run would use does not fit''.

\textbf{The real limit, however, is time, not memory.} At 258 seconds per step and batch size 1, repeating our two-epoch presence training (22{,}800 training windows, 45{,}600 examples seen in total) would take about \textbf{136 days} of non-stop computing. The DistilBERT run it would replace took about 50 minutes. Even if we allow for extra start-up time in a short test (our own DistilBERT step takes 3.17\,s here but about 1.0\,s during the real run, so these numbers may be about three times too high), the conclusion does not change: a third of 136 days is still 45 days, and the project had weeks, not months.

\textbf{The cost when the app runs is also confirmed by measurement.} Scanning one typical contract (33{,}143 characters) for all 41 categories takes 22 windows $\times$ 41 $\times$ 11.7\,ms $\approx$ \textbf{10.6 seconds} with our DistilBERT, compared with 3 windows $\times$ 41 $\times$ 3.69\,s $\approx$ \textbf{454 seconds} (seven and a half minutes) with Longformer. Clausify is meant to respond within seconds, and this measurement rules Longformer out for the app, whatever a large training computer could do.

None of this shows that a Longformer would have scored worse. It shows only that we could not have found out on this hardware, which is a much narrower claim than the one we started with. Settling the modelling question needs a CUDA machine, and we list it as future work instead of pretending the cost argument answers it.

\subsection{The Clausify Application}
\label{sec:clausify}
\subsubsection{Purpose and Design}
The final result of the project is \emph{Clausify}, a Streamlit web app that runs all of the project's trained models on any contract the user gives it (the DistilBERT presence classifier, the TF--IDF baseline for the optional AND-ensemble, the span model and the FLAN-T5 summarizer) and shows the results as a report sorted by risk. It is the system of Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}, made easy to use. The design goal is simple: a non-lawyer should see the ``watch out before signing'' items first. Clauses found are grouped \textcolor{riskhigh}{\textbf{High}} $\rightarrow$ \textcolor{riskmed}{\textbf{Medium}} $\rightarrow$ \textcolor{risklow}{\textbf{Low}} and sorted by model score within each group.
\subsubsection{User Workflow}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Add a contract.} Paste the text, or upload a \texttt{.pdf}, \texttt{.docx} or \texttt{.txt} file; the text is read on the user's own computer (Figure~\ref{fig:apphome}). The user picks \emph{Whole contract}, \emph{Separate clauses} (Section~\ref{sec:clausemode}) or \emph{Auto}. A settings panel chooses the detection rule (DistilBERT alone, the default, or the AND-ensemble) and turns the plain-English lines on or off. Detection uses the fixed 0.5 threshold used in all our tests.
  \item \textbf{Analyze.} One click runs all the models; a progress bar follows the scan of the 41 categories and then the summarizer.
  \item \textbf{Read the report.} A summary bar counts the clauses found at each risk level (Figure~\ref{fig:appresults}), and below it each clause appears as a card. On the made-up test contract the bar shows 7 High, 14 Medium and 9 Low risk clauses: 30 of the 41 categories found (October 2026 models, DistilBERT detection).
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{How the Models Run Inside the App}
The app follows exactly the same steps that were used in testing:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Windows.} The contract is cut into 2{,}000-character windows that move forward 1{,}500 characters at a time, the same as in training. At most 30 windows are read, so very long documents stay fast.
  \item \textbf{Model 1: presence.} For each of the 41 categories, the exact CUAD question the model was trained on is paired with every window and scored by the window-level DistilBERT. The score for the whole contract is the highest window score; categories above the threshold are reported as present, and the best window is remembered.
  \item \textbf{Finding the clause.} Inside that best window, the presence model scores each paragraph against the category question, and the highest-scoring paragraph is quoted on the card; the DistilBERT question-answering model then highlights the key phrase inside it. An earlier version let the span model choose the text from the whole window, and on test inputs it returned the same sentence for almost every category: the span model prefers text near the position where answers were during training (Section~\ref{sec:e2e}), so it is now used only inside the paragraph the presence model chose.
  \item \textbf{Optional AND-ensemble.} With this setting, the TF--IDF baseline scores the whole contract, and a category is reported only when both models' scores reach 0.5, exactly as tested in Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap}.
  \item \textbf{Risk layer.} The category found is looked up in the fixed risk table (8 High, 22 Medium, 11 Low) for its level and reason.
  \item \textbf{Summarizer.} FLAN-T5-small receives each quoted clause with the same prompt and settings as in testing and returns one plain-English sentence.
\end{enumerate}

All the models run on the CPU, so the app works on any computer; a typical contract takes about 27 seconds once the models are loaded (Table~\ref{tab:appbench}). The app loads the actual trained models saved by the training code (\texttt{presence\_mil}, \texttt{span}, \texttt{summarizer} and the TF--IDF \texttt{baseline.pkl}). When they are not on the computer, as on a cloud server, it downloads the same files once from a public Hugging Face model repository.
\subsubsection{Implementation Notes}
The app is a single-page Streamlit program (\texttt{app.py}) built on two small modules: \texttt{model\_utils.py}, which loads the models and handles windows, scoring, clause finding, the summarizer and the risk table, and \texttt{features.py}, which reads uploaded files, builds the highlighted view and creates the reports. Two test contracts come with the app: a real CUAD contract, and a made-up ``Master Software License and Distribution Agreement'' that we wrote to cover many of the 41 categories. On that made-up contract the app finds clauses at all three risk levels and puts High-risk clauses such as \emph{Liquidated Damages}, \emph{Exclusivity}, \emph{IP Ownership Assignment} and \emph{Uncapped Liability} at the top of the report: exactly the before-you-sign warnings the design aims for.
\subsubsection{Clause-by-Clause Mode}
\label{sec:clausemode}
Users often paste a few clauses instead of a whole contract and expect one card per clause. Whole-contract mode does not do this: for each of the 41 categories it asks whether that category appears anywhere in the window, so six related clauses pasted together gave fourteen categories, eight of them wrong. So the app has a second mode (chosen automatically for a short list of paragraphs, or chosen by the user) in which each paragraph is one clause and gets exactly one category and one risk level. When most paragraphs are numbered (``1.'', ``4.2'', ``(a)'', ``Section 5''), only the numbered ones are treated as clauses; the title, opening paragraph and signature lines are listed separately as skipped, so a contract with 18 numbered clauses gives exactly 18 cards, numbered as in the contract.

In this mode, if a clause heading names a category (``\textsc{2. Exclusivity.}''), that heading decides the category directly; headings that do not clearly name one category are ignored. Otherwise the presence model scores the clause against all 41 category questions. The raw scores cannot simply be compared across categories (\emph{Parties}, for example, scores about 0.98 on almost any clause that names the two sides). So each category's score is adjusted by how that category scores on real CUAD clauses of \emph{other} categories, using 1{,}586 clauses from the training split, and the clause gets the category with the highest adjusted score. A clause whose best adjusted score is below 2 is shown as \emph{unrecognized} instead of being forced into a category.

Tested on 721 clauses from the \emph{test} split, this adjustment raises the share of clauses given the right category from 9.6\% (raw highest score) to \textbf{42.3\%} out of 41 categories. The right category is in the top three 65.3\% of the time, and the right risk level is given 67.0\% of the time. This is well below the whole-contract scores, which is expected: the presence model was trained to scan 2{,}000-character windows, not to label single clauses. The app shows these numbers under every clause-mode result. On the six-clause example above, the version with headings is labelled correctly (five High, one Low). With the headings removed, the model alone gets none of the six risk levels right (it labels five of them Medium and leaves one unrecognized). So for inputs like this, the clause headings provide the accuracy, and cards decided by the model alone should be checked against the clause text.
