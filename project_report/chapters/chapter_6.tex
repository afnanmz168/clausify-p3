\section{Performance Evaluation}
Each step gets the score that suits its task. Deciding \emph{which clauses are present} is a yes/no decision for each of the 4{,}182 (contract, category) test pairs, and we score it with micro-F1 (every decision counts the same), macro-F1 (every category counts the same), and the accuracy, precision, recall and specificity from the confusion matrix. \emph{Finding the clause text} is scored with token-level F1, exact match, and the share of answers that overlap the real clause with F1~$\geq$~0.5. \emph{Summaries} are scored with ROUGE-L against two different sets of reference sentences. For the \emph{whole system} we count the share of real clauses that get through every step, and for the risk layer we count how many clauses it finds at each risk level. Speed is the time and memory the app needs (Table~\ref{tab:appbench}).

Every model is tested once on the same 102 test contracts, with a separate notebook that loads the saved models from disk. We measure uncertainty with a bootstrap over contracts. We also check whether the main result moves when we change the amount of training data, the random seed, the way window scores are combined, the decision threshold or the train/test split.

All results in this chapter come from the 20\% test set, which is the same 102 unseen contracts for every model. The numbers are from the October 2026 re-training (Chapter~4) unless a run is marked as August 2026. A separate test notebook produced them by loading the trained models from disk, so nothing left over from training can affect them.
\subsection{Presence Classification}
\subsubsection{Model comparison}
Table~\ref{tab:ensemble} and Figure~\ref{fig:ensemble} compare the presence models on the 4{,}182 (contract, category) test decisions. On its own, the window-based transformer loses to the simple TF--IDF model that reads the whole document (0.692 vs.\ 0.775). Legal wording is distinctive, so word counts over the whole contract carry a lot of signal, and taking the highest window score raises too many false alarms, as Section~\ref{sec:mil} predicted. The AND-ensemble, which reports a clause only when both models agree, removes many of these false alarms and comes first (0.779). Its lead over the simple model is small for 102 test contracts, and Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap} shows that the gap cannot be told apart from zero. What we take from the table is a point about method: a simple word model is already strong at deciding which clauses a contract contains, and the transformer helps most as a filter that removes false alarms.

\subsection{Span Extraction}
\label{sec:span}
We tested the question-answering model on all 2{,}667 test windows that contain an answer (Table~\ref{tab:span}). It reaches a token-F1 of 0.764, and 82.8\% of its answers overlap the real clause at F1 $\geq$ 0.5. So when the clause is in front of the model, it captures most of the right text about eight times in ten. Exact match is lower, which we expected: marked legal clauses are long, and their exact start and end are often debatable (should the section number at the start be included?), so overlap is the more useful score here. For comparison, the best published CUAD result uses DeBERTa-xlarge, which has about ten times as many parameters as our 67M-parameter model.

A score on 102 contracts needs a confidence interval next to it (Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap}), so we ran the same bootstrap over contracts here. The clauses are even more linked to each other than in the presence task. The 2{,}667 span decisions come from only 102 contracts, and a contract with unusual writing (numbered sub-clauses, many defined terms) pulls all of its clauses in the same direction. Table~\ref{tab:span} therefore gives two intervals for each score. One resamples whole \emph{contracts}, which is the correct method. The other resamples single \emph{clauses}, which is the naive method, and we show it because the difference between the two is informative.

Resampling contracts gives a token-F1 interval of $[0.743, 0.782]$, roughly twice as wide as the $[0.752, 0.775]$ we would get by treating clauses as independent. The extra width is what it costs to ignore that clauses from one contract are linked, the same effect that made the contract-level bootstrap necessary in Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap}. It also tells us how precise the score is. On a test set this size, differences smaller than about two points of token-F1 cannot be measured, so 0.764 should be compared with published figures with that margin in mind.

This test has a limit. It measures extraction when the window contains the answer. More exactly, each test window is the 1{,}200-character focus window placed around the real answer (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}), so the model is indirectly told where to look. The result is a best case, and the real system cannot reach it. The CUAD paper's full-document test (precision at 80\% recall over all windows, including those with no answer) needs a heavier ranking setup that we did not build, so we make no claim about that score. Section~\ref{sec:e2e} looks at the other side: how extraction does when the presence step chooses the window.
\subsection{Summarization}
\label{sec:summ}

\subsubsection{What the reference set actually is}
One fact about this test decides what its score can mean, so we state it first. CUAD has no plain-English versions of its clauses, so there was no ready-made set of target sentences to train or test with. We wrote the targets ourselves: 41 one-sentence summaries, one per category, written from the weaker party's side (Section~\ref{sec:summmethod}). Every training pair and every test pair uses one of these 41 sentences, and the target never changes from clause to clause. Two \emph{Cap on Liability} clauses with very different limits have exactly the same target sentence.

This choice has a side effect. With 41 different targets for 2{,}667 test clauses, a model can score well by recognising the category and repeating its sentence, and the score cannot tell this apart from real summarizing. The test below separates the two.

\subsubsection{Two reference sets, the same 150 clauses}
We test the fine-tuned FLAN-T5-small on 150 test clauses (chosen with seed 42) against two sets of reference sentences:

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item Template references: the category template, exactly as in training. The main score was produced this way.
  \item Clause-specific references: 150 one-sentence summaries, one per clause, each describing that particular clause with its actual limit, notice period and parties. A large language model helped draft them from the clause text alone, without seeing any model output, and they are fully separate from the 41 templates. They are a trial reference set that no lawyer wrote, and we claim nothing more for them.
\end{enumerate}

The clauses are the same in both tests, so the gap between the two scores measures one thing: how much of the ROUGE-L score comes from the reference sentences all being the same. Table~\ref{tab:sumeval} gives the result. The summarizer re-trained in October 2026 gives exactly the same scores as the original.

\subsubsection{Reading the table}
We draw three conclusions from the table.

First, the 0.775 comes from copying templates, and the numbers show it. For all 150 test clauses the fine-tuned model outputs one of the 41 template sentences word for word: 100\% of outputs are an exact copy of a template, the right one 70\% of the time and another category's template the other 30\%. The model never writes a sentence of its own. When it is wrong, it is wrong the way a classifier is wrong; a \emph{Price Restrictions} clause about a 10\% rate cap gets the \emph{ROFR} template. Fine-tuning taught the model to pick one of 41 sentences.

Second, the last row of Table~\ref{tab:sumeval} shows that a lookup table does as well. The category template alone, with no model at all, scores 0.115 against the clause-specific references, which matches the fine-tuned model's 0.116 within the margin of error. Whatever the fine-tuned summarizer learned adds nothing we can measure beyond printing the category's sentence.

Third, fine-tuning made the model worse at the real task. Against the clause-specific references, the original model (before fine-tuning) scores 0.299, more than twice the fine-tuned model's 0.116. The original FLAN-T5 is still a poor legal summarizer. As Table~\ref{tab:sumqual} shows, it mostly repeats the source text, and repeating the source raises a word-overlap score when the reference uses the same words as the clause. What we can conclude is narrower and still unwelcome: fine-tuning removed the clause-specific content the original model had and replaced it with smooth, general sentences, which score well only against references made of those same sentences.

\subsubsection{What we therefore claim}
What we can claim is a working ``sort into a category, then show a template'' system. It finds a clause's category and shows a plain-English sentence for that category, written in advance by people. In an app that still helps: a signer who reads ``You cannot transfer this contract to someone else without permission'' next to a block of legal text is better off than before. (Clausify shows this line on every card, labelled as the summarizer's output and placed next to the fixed reason from the risk table; when the sentence belongs to another category, the card says so; Section~\ref{sec:clausify}.) We make no claim of free-text legal summarizing, and the 0.775 should not be used as evidence for one. The number that describes how well the model summarizes single clauses is 0.116, so this part of the project is a proof of concept.

Going further needs target sentences that differ from clause to clause. The trial set above is a small sample of exactly that, and its 150 references took a few hours to draft. Section~\ref{sec:futurework} describes a larger version.

\section{Analysis of Design Solutions}
This section looks at the design as a whole: how many clauses get through the full system, whether its mistakes land where they would hurt a signer, what the mistakes look like, and how the ensemble handles rare categories.

\subsection{End-to-End: What Actually Reaches the User}
\label{sec:e2e}

So far every number measured one step with the other steps out of the way. Presence was scored on its own, extraction was scored with a window already known to contain the answer, and summaries were scored on real clauses. That is the right way to test each part, but it does not tell us what the system does to a contract. The steps run in a chain and errors add up: a category the presence step misses is a clause the span step never sees.

We therefore ran the whole system on all 102 test contracts and asked, for each of the 1{,}348 real clauses, whether it gets through all three steps. A clause counts only if (i) the presence step finds its category, (ii) the span step, reading the window the presence step chose (never a window chosen with the real answer), returns text that overlaps the real clause with token-F1 $\geq$ 0.5, and (iii) the risk level given is the correct one. Table~\ref{tab:e2e} shows how many clauses are lost at each step.

The risk step loses nothing. The level is a fixed lookup for each category, so it is right exactly when the category is right and adds no errors of its own. This follows from the design. ``Correct'' also only means ``matches our risk table'', which no expert has checked yet (Section~\ref{sec:taxvalid}).

\subsubsection{Where the clauses are lost}
The big loss happens at the span step, and it has two separate causes. We told them apart by checking, for every clause whose category was found, whether the window chosen by the presence model actually contained the clause.

The first cause is that the window with the highest score is often the wrong window. We chose the maximum rule (Section~\ref{sec:mil}) because it removes the search limit: if any window fires, the category is reported. Section~\ref{sec:mil} already discussed what that costs in precision. We had not expected the second cost. The highest-scoring window is also used to locate the clause, and it is poor at that job, missing the clause entirely 30.9\% of the time. The model was never trained to locate clauses. It was trained to answer ``does this window contain a clause of this category?'', and a window that talks about liability in general can score higher than the one that sets the limit. For those 327 clauses the span step gets the wrong 2{,}000 characters, and no extraction model could recover them.

The second cause is that the span model is much weaker on windows unlike the ones it was trained on. Even when the window does contain the clause, extraction succeeds only 39.6\% of the time, against 82.8\% in the separate test of Section~\ref{sec:span}. The focus windows explain this (Section~\ref{sec:spanmethod}). In training, every example was centred so that the answer started about 150 characters into a 1{,}200-character window. This is allowed, because the answer is used to build a training example and never as an input when the system runs (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}). It still had a cost. The model has only seen windows with the answer near the start, while the real system gives it plain 1{,}200-character chunks where the answer can be anywhere, and the average token-F1 falls from 0.764 to 0.383. The centring did not leak the test set, but it narrowed the kind of text the model can handle.

\subsubsection{What this changes}
This changes how the earlier results should be read in three ways.

The span scores in Section~\ref{sec:span} are an upper limit on real extraction quality. They answer ``how good is the extractor when given the right window?'', which is a fair question about the model and a misleading one about the system.

A bigger model is also not the most useful next step. The second cause is a data problem with a cheap fix: train the span model on windows where the answer sits at different positions, or on the same plain chunks it sees in real use. The first cause needs a model trained to choose the extraction window, instead of reusing the presence score. Both fit within this project's computing budget, and neither needs more parameters.

Clausify's output should be read as a shortlist. When the system shows a clause card, the category is right about 78\% of the time the clause was present, but in this test the span model's quoted text was a good copy of the real clause only about one time in five overall. The app has since changed how it picks the quote. It now quotes the whole paragraph that the presence model scores highest, which fixed the clear failures we saw in our own tests, but we have not measured this method on the test set. The app tells users that it is not legal advice and that the quoted text may not be the exact clause the badge refers to (Section~\ref{sec:demolimits}).

We had not asked this before, and the answer reverses the recommendation that the overall score gives.

\subsubsection{The headline configuration is the worst one for High-risk clauses}
Start with the High level. The AND-ensemble, which we report as our main setup because of its overall micro-F1, misses 36.9\% of High-risk clauses. The transformer alone, the weaker model in the previous sections, misses 15.9\%. The more permissive OR rule comes last on overall micro-F1 (0.692, tied with the transformer alone) and misses only 14.2\%.

Out of 176 High-risk clauses in 102 contracts, the ensemble finds 111 and misses 65, while OR finds 151 and misses 25. Choosing the ensemble over OR for its micro-F1 costs a signer 40 High-risk clauses (unlimited liabilities, IP assignments, non-competes). In return the ensemble gets a ranking that Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap} already showed is a statistical tie.

The cause is the one found in Section~\ref{sec:raretail}, and here it adds up. Several High-risk categories are uncommon. \emph{Most Favored Nation} is among the ten rarest categories, and \emph{Liquidated Damages} (11 test examples) and \emph{Irrevocable or Perpetual License} (17) are also below average. The AND rule is hardest to satisfy where training data is thinnest, so it hurts recall most at the risk level that needs recall most. The Low level shows the opposite picture. Of the 1{,}348 real clauses, 674 are Low-risk (dates, party names, governing law), and every model scores about 0.9 on them, so the clauses that matter least dominate the overall micro-F1.

\subsubsection{What we conclude, and what we do not}
We do not change the reported setup because of this table. Picking OR after seeing which rule does best on the High level of the test set would mean choosing a setup with the test set, which Section~\ref{sec:valprotocol} says this project avoided. The table is a diagnosis, and we leave the tuning to a proper validation split.

It does show that our evaluation score never matched our stated goal, and that the mismatch is large enough to reverse the ranking of the models. A system meant to warn a signer before signing should be chosen by how many High-risk clauses it finds, or by a risk-weighted F1. An average over 41 categories, where half of the real clauses are Low-risk ones such as dates and party names, is the wrong yardstick. Section~\ref{sec:futurework} describes the proper version of this test: decide the risk-weighted goal first, choose the rule on a separate validation split, and report on the test set once.

The table also depends on Section~\ref{sec:taxvalid}. Every number in Table~\ref{tab:riskeval} uses our own choice of which categories are High, Medium and Low. If a lawyer disagrees about which categories are High, the table changes with it. The risk-weighted test carries all the warnings about the unchecked risk table and adds no independent evidence for it.

\subsection{Does the AND Rule Hurt Rare Categories More Than Common Ones?}
\label{sec:raretail}
Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap} supports the AND-ensemble for the kind of mistakes it makes, since it turns the transformer's many false alarms into balanced precision and recall. That argument rests on the overall numbers, and an overall number can hide a trade that falls unevenly across categories. The rare categories are the obvious place to look. Agreement between two models is hardest to get where each model has the least training data, and agreement is exactly what the rule requires.

We sorted the 41 categories by how often they appear in the training data and took the ten rarest (11 to 42 positive training contracts, 70 positive test decisions in total). On the rare categories the ensemble gains nothing. Every point of precision is paid for with recall. The transformer alone finds 39 of the 70 rare clauses, and requiring agreement cuts this to 18. On these categories the TF--IDF model alone is now the weakest of the three (F1 0.353), yet its vetoes decide what the ensemble reports.

The per-category details are clearer than the average. On five of the ten rarest categories the AND rule scores exactly 0.000. On three of those (\emph{Price Restrictions}, \emph{Unlimited/All-You-Can-Eat License}, \emph{No-Solicit of Customers}) the transformer alone was finding something (F1 0.087, 0.500, 0.323) before the AND rule removed it. On these categories the rule throws away clauses that one of the models had found.

The overall micro-F1 counts all 4{,}182 decisions equally, and a signer does not. Missing a rare clause is not clearly cheaper than missing a common one, and it may be much worse. \emph{Most Favored Nation} is among the ten rarest categories, it is one of our eight High-risk categories, and both models score 0.000 on it. A rule that favours precision on common categories, even one with no settings, improves the score without reducing harm. We report the ensemble as our main setup because the overall test chooses it. On this evidence, though, a system meant to protect signers would do better with the more permissive rule for High-risk categories and the AND rule for the rest, accepting more false alarms where a missed clause costs the most. We have not built that mixed rule. Building and testing it properly is future work (Section~\ref{sec:futurework}), and adding it after looking at the test set would not be a fair test.

\section{Final Design Adjustments}
Two choices made before training were never tuned: max-pooling as the way to combine window scores, and 0.5 as the decision threshold. After the main tests we went back to both to see whether changing them would change the conclusions or the app.

\subsection{Was Max-Pooling the Right Way to Combine Window Scores?}
\label{sec:pooling}
Section~\ref{sec:mil} chose max-pooling because it is the multiple-instance rule \cite{maron1998mil}: a bag is positive if any one of its items is. Sections~\ref{sec:mil} and~\ref{sec:budget} then blamed max-pooling for the transformer's many false alarms. Neither statement had been tested against another rule, which is a weak basis for a claim the project depends on. We therefore scored every window once with the October 2026 model and compared six ways of combining the same window scores, so that the rule is the only thing that changes. As a check, max-pooling at 0.5 gives exactly the 0.6924 of the main test.

Three results come out of this, and together they correct an earlier claim of ours.

At the threshold we actually used, max-pooling was not the best rule. At 0.5, the mean of the three highest window scores reaches 0.7115, against 0.6924 for the maximum. That recovers almost a quarter of the gap to the baseline from a one-line change. Averaging the top three windows is a softer version of the multiple-instance rule: it still looks for evidence concentrated somewhere in the document, but it wants that evidence in more than one place, which guards against the single false window that Section~\ref{sec:mil} named as max-pooling's weakness. The mean over all windows fails at 0.5 (recall 0.090) for an obvious reason. An average over about 35 windows, one of them positive, cannot reach 0.5, so its best threshold is just 0.10.

Once each rule gets its own threshold, max-pooling is the best of the six. At 0.90 it reaches 0.7529, ahead of 0.7253 for the top-3 mean. Its scores just do not suit a line drawn at 0.5, which is what Section~\ref{sec:threshold} finds from the other direction. Much of the transformer's gap is about where the line is drawn, and less about how window scores are combined. The last column of Table~\ref{tab:pooling} shows the same inside the AND-ensemble: with max-pooling the ensemble scores 0.7789, and every other rule does worse.

No rule reaches the baseline. The best value in the whole table (max-pooling at a threshold chosen on the test set, already a generous upper limit) is 0.7529, against 0.7747 for the baseline, and every other rule stays behind at every threshold we tried. This is the most important of the three results because it removes the last explanation left for RQ1. The window-based transformer loses whatever we change: its training data (Section~\ref{sec:budget}), its seed (Section~\ref{sec:seeds}), its way of combining windows or its threshold.

This corrects Section~\ref{sec:budget}. There we wrote that ``the limit here is the way window scores are combined, not the model underneath it'', judging from how precision and recall moved when the training data was doubled. Table~\ref{tab:pooling} shows that this was wrong. At the 0.5 threshold the best other rule gains only 0.019 micro-F1, and even the best pair of rule and threshold found on the test set gains 0.061 and stays below the baseline. The way window scores are combined matters, but it is a secondary factor. We left the original sentence in place with this correction attached, as we do elsewhere in this chapter, because a reader learns more from a guess that was later measured than from one that was silently changed.

This test has limits. It changes only the rule applied to the window scores. It does not change the window size (2{,}000 characters, step 1{,}500), which would need new windows and retraining, and it does not try a learned rule such as attention pooling over the windows. Both remain untested. Attention pooling is the obvious next step, since it could learn to weigh windows by how relevant they are.
\subsection{The Threshold and What the Confidence Score Means}
\label{sec:threshold}

Every presence decision in this project, and every clause card in Clausify, comes from comparing a score with 0.5. Section~\ref{sec:valprotocol} notes that nobody chose this threshold; it is the default for a two-class model. Two questions follow. Is 0.5 a good place to draw the line, and does the score being compared with it mean anything? Figure~\ref{fig:threshcal} answers both.

\subsubsection{0.5 is a poor operating point for the transformer}
Trying other thresholds after the fact, the transformer reaches a micro-F1 of 0.753 at a threshold of 0.9, against 0.692 at the default. The gain of 0.061 closes most of its gap with the TF--IDF baseline (0.775). Section~\ref{sec:mil} predicted the reason: the maximum over about 35 windows is pushed upwards by design, so the scores bunch up near 1 and a line at 0.5 lets far too much through. Precision is 0.558 at 0.5 and 0.726 at 0.9.

We are not switching to 0.9. It was found by looking at the test set, and using it would turn a held-out test into a tuned one, which Section~\ref{sec:valprotocol} says this project avoided. What we can say is that part of the transformer's gap comes from a threshold nobody chose, and part comes from how well it tells clauses apart. The comparison in Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap} therefore sets a baseline whose scores already suit 0.5 against a transformer whose scores do not. A fair rematch would choose each model's threshold on a validation split taken from the 408 training contracts, and we did not do that. Section~\ref{sec:budget} showed that the gap is not caused by too little training data. This section shows that part of it may come from the threshold, and that warning belongs next to the finding.

The AND-ensemble behaves the other way. Its best threshold is already 0.5 (0.779), and raising it makes things much worse. At 0.7 micro-F1 falls to 0.611 and High-risk recall to 0.165, because asking both models to pass a high bar is close to asking both to be very sure at once. The dashed curves in Figure~\ref{fig:threshcal} show the conflict. For the transformer, the threshold with the best micro-F1 (0.9) lowers High-risk recall from 0.841 to 0.716, so no single threshold is best for both the overall score and the clauses that matter most.

\subsubsection{The confidence bar in the application is not a probability}
Clausify shows the presence score to the user as a confidence bar. A reliability diagram tests whether the bar means what a user would think it means, and it does not.

The transformer's score has an Expected Calibration Error of 0.205 and a Brier score of 0.195, and it is too confident across the whole range. Among predictions between 0.5 and 0.6 the clause is really there only 16.5\% of the time. Even in the top group (scores above 0.9, which the app shows as an almost full bar) it is there 72.6\% of the time, so a user who reads ``96\% confident'' is looking at about a 73\% chance. Max-pooling is again the cause. The maximum over dozens of windows is not a probability, and nothing in training asked it to be one.

The TF--IDF baseline is also off (ECE 0.172), mostly in the safer direction: in the middle range it is less confident than it should be. Its predictions between 0.7 and 0.8 are right 89.6\% of the time, and those between 0.8 and 0.9 are right 97.8\% of the time.

For the app this means the score on every card is not a probability of being right, which we record in Section~\ref{sec:demolimits}. The fix needs no retraining. Platt scaling or isotonic regression, fitted on a validation split, would turn the scores into real probabilities, and it is the cheapest single improvement to the interface. We had no validation split set aside to fit it on, which is a consequence of the gap described in Section~\ref{sec:valprotocol}.

\section{Statistical Analysis}
Our main statistical tool is a paired bootstrap over contracts \cite{efron1979bootstrap}. We resample the 102 test contracts with replacement 10{,}000 times and score every model on the same resample, so the 41 linked decisions from each contract stay together. We then measure three sources of variation the bootstrap cannot see: the amount of training data, the random seed and the train/test split.

\subsection{How Much of That Ranking Is Real?}
\label{sec:bootstrap}
Table~\ref{tab:ensemble} is a ranking, and readers tend to treat the order of a ranking as a result. A lead of 0.779 over 0.775 on 102 test contracts is about four decisions in a thousand, too small to take at face value, so we measured it.

A bootstrap confidence interval is the natural tool, but what we resample matters. The 4{,}182 test decisions are not independent. Each contract contributes 41 of them, and an unusual contract (very long, or oddly written) moves all 41 together. Resampling single (contract, category) decisions would ignore this and give intervals that are far too narrow. We therefore resample whole contracts with replacement (a cluster bootstrap): we draw 102 contracts from the 102 we have, recompute micro-F1 for both models, and repeat 10{,}000 times. Both models are scored on the same resample each time, so the difference is paired, and its interval is much narrower than the two separate intervals would suggest.

The three comparisons come out differently.

The ensemble's win over the baseline is not proven. The interval is $[-0.0037,\allowbreak +0.0115]$, which includes zero, and the ensemble is ahead in 85.5\% of resamples, more often than not but well short of the 97.5\% a clear win would need. On their own the two models score 0.779 (95\% CI $[0.762, 0.795]$) and 0.775 (95\% CI $[0.758, 0.791]$), and the two intervals almost completely overlap. On this test set we cannot tell the AND-ensemble and the TF--IDF baseline apart. This is a measurement, and the top two rows of Table~\ref{tab:ensemble} should be read as a tie.

The transformer's loss is real. The baseline beats the window-based transformer by 0.0824, with an interval of $[+0.0647, +0.1013]$ that is nowhere near zero, and it wins in all 10{,}000 resamples. So the project's two main claims stand on different ground. The finding we trust most, that a whole-document word model beats a window-based DistilBERT at deciding which clauses a contract contains, passes the test easily. The finding we were already careful about, that the AND-ensemble leads the baseline, does not pass. Our caution pointed the right way, and now it has been checked.

The case for the ensemble never rested on 0.004 of micro-F1, so it survives this. The AND rule earns its place by the kind of mistakes it makes: it turns the transformer's many false alarms into balanced precision and recall (Table~\ref{tab:metrics}). Sections~\ref{sec:seeds} and~\ref{sec:raretail} look at what this costs.
\subsection{How Much Changes If We Only Change the Random Seed?}
\label{sec:seeds}
Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap} measured how much the score depends on which contracts ended up in the test set. A second kind of uncertainty is invisible to it. Every result so far comes from one training run per model, and training is partly random: the starting weights of the classifier, the order of the data and the sample of negative windows all depend on the seed. If changing only the seed moves the score as much as the effects we discuss, a ranking built on single runs is reporting noise.

This is easy to check. We trained the presence transformer three times with 24{,}000 training examples, changing only the seed (42, 43 and 44), and tested each run on the same 102 contracts. Seed 42 is the October 2026 run used in the rest of this chapter; seeds 43 and 44 were trained in August 2026 with the same settings. Table~\ref{tab:seeds} shows the spread.

Seed noise does not explain the ensemble's lead, but other noise can. The AND-ensemble leads the baseline by $+0.0042$. The bootstrap interval still includes zero (Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap}), and a different train/test split moves the baseline by $0.0063$ (Section~\ref{sec:splitvar}), 1.5 times the lead, so we still read the top two rows as a tie.

Seed noise does not touch the finding we rely on. The baseline beats the transformer by 0.0824, which is 9.2 times the transformer's seed standard deviation of 0.0090. Even the luckiest seed, at 0.7082, still loses to the baseline by 0.0665. No seed makes the window-based transformer competitive. Section~\ref{sec:budget} shows that more than doubling the training data does not close the gap either, which leaves the design of the model as the explanation.

The noisier model is the transformer alone, which varies about six times as much across seeds ($\mathrm{SD} = 0.0090$) as the ensemble that contains it ($0.0015$). Requiring agreement with a fixed word model calms the transformer's run-to-run changes along with its false alarms. A false detection has to get past a second opinion that never changes, so most of the seed-driven changes in what the transformer reports are filtered out. This steadiness is a real property of the AND rule, although Section~\ref{sec:raretail} shows it costs recall on rare categories, and it is a better argument for the ensemble than its rank.

This check covers only the presence transformer. The span extractor and the summarizer were each trained twice with the same seed (August and October 2026) and gave the same scores to within 0.001 (token-F1 0.763 and 0.764; ROUGE-L 0.775 both times). That limits run-to-run noise on this hardware but says nothing about seed variance, which is still unmeasured for those two models. We list it in Section~\ref{sec:threats} instead of assuming it is small.
\subsection{Was the Transformer Simply Undertrained?}
\label{sec:budget}
One objection to Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap} cannot be answered by the bootstrap, and it concerns the project's main claim. The TF--IDF baseline is trained on all 408 training contracts. The window-based transformer used a balanced sample of 24{,}000 of the 53{,}662 available training windows, to keep training possible on a laptop (Section~\ref{sec:prestrain}). When the transformer loses by about 0.08 micro-F1, the comparison alone cannot say whether max-pooling over windows is weaker for this task or whether the transformer simply never saw 55\% of its own training data. The first would be a finding about the method and the second a side effect of our computing budget, and they lead to very different conclusions.

To settle this, in August 2026 we retrained the presence transformer on all 53{,}662 training windows and kept everything else the same: windows, negative sampling, settings, seed and test. The run took 140.7 minutes on the same laptop, against about 50 minutes for the smaller version. Table~\ref{tab:budget} shows the result. Both runs in the table are from August 2026, so they are compared with each other and not with the October numbers.

Too little training data does not explain the gap. Using 124\% more training data changed micro-F1 by $-0.0004$, with an interval of $[-0.0098, +0.0095]$ that sits across zero, so the two budgets cannot be told apart. The gap to the baseline also stayed put: $+0.0809$ with all the data against $+0.0805$ with the sample (August runs), with an interval nowhere near zero and the baseline winning in all 10{,}000 resamples. Whatever holds the window-based transformer back, it is not a shortage of training examples.

How the extra data changed things is still informative. Recall rose from 0.9080 to 0.9266 and precision fell from 0.5620 to 0.5546, so more training data made the model report clauses more readily without making it more accurate. Section~\ref{sec:mil} predicted this weakness of max-pooling. With about 35 windows per contract, the decision for the whole document is a maximum over many chances to be wrong, and a better-trained window scorer that is still imperfect produces more false maxima. The limit here is the way window scores are combined, not the model underneath it.\footnote{This judgement turned out to be only partly right, and Section~\ref{sec:pooling} corrects it: with the other five rules, at the 0.5 threshold the best gain is only 0.019 micro-F1, and no rule at any threshold reaches the baseline, so the way window scores are combined is a secondary factor, not the main limit.}

We also saw a smaller effect that leaves our conclusion unchanged. The AND-ensemble built on the full-data transformer scores 0.7820, and its lead over the baseline is $+0.0073$ with a 95\% interval of $[-0.0000, +0.0140]$. It wins in 97.4\% of resamples, but the interval still touches zero, so more training data moves the ensemble's lead from a clear tie to a borderline one. We do not claim it, because an interval whose lower end sits on zero does not pass the bar we set in Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap}, and we will not lower the bar because the number moved our way. The ensemble's balance between precision and recall stays the same with more data.

The rest of this chapter (the confusion matrix, the per-category results and the whole-system test of Section~\ref{sec:e2e}) and the model in Clausify all use the October 2026 model trained on 24{,}000 examples. The full-data run is a control experiment that tests one possible explanation, and we report it that way instead of swapping in a slightly better number afterwards. The two cannot be told apart, so nothing later would change if we had.
\subsection{A Third Source of Variation: The Split Itself}
\label{sec:splitvar}
Sections~\ref{sec:budget} and~\ref{sec:seeds} tested two things that could move our numbers, the amount of training data and the random seed, and neither changed the conclusions. A third source may be the largest of all: the split. Every result in this project comes from one contract-level 80/20 split made with seed 42. With only 510 contracts, a different split puts different rare categories in the test set and could move the scores by itself. Seed variance does not capture this, because changing the training seed keeps the test set the same.

Retraining the transformer on twenty splits is too expensive, but the TF--IDF baseline retrains in under a minute. Every comparison in this project is made against it, so its spread across splits shows how far the comparison itself could move. We made 20 separate contract-level 80/20 splits and retrained the baseline on each.

The split is the largest of the three uncertainties we measured. Its standard deviation is 0.0063, against 0.0015 for the ensemble's seed variance and a bootstrap half-width of about 0.008 for the comparison. That is 1.5 times the ensemble's $+0.0042$ lead, so a different split could easily remove the lead, which gives one more reason to treat it as unproven.

Our split is not a lucky one. At 0.7747 the baseline sits below the 20-split mean of 0.7829, in the lower part of the range, so if anything this project reports the baseline on a slightly unfavourable split. A split that happened to favour the baseline would have been a real worry, since it would make the transformer's loss look worse than it is.

The finding we rely on is far outside this variation. The baseline's lead over the transformer is 0.0824, about thirteen split-level standard deviations, and no realistic new split would reverse it.

We can say nothing about how stable single categories are across splits, and that is where the split would matter most. A rare category with two test examples in one split may have eight in another, and its F1 means little in either case. Repeated splits or cross-validation for the whole system remain future work, and Section~\ref{sec:limitations} lists this as a limitation.

\section{Discussions}
The results of this chapter fall into three groups.

Three findings survived every check we could run.
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item For deciding which clauses a contract contains, a word-based baseline that reads the whole document beats a window-based DistilBERT by $+0.082$ micro-F1. None of the other explanations we tested held up: training on all 53{,}662 windows instead of 24{,}000 changed the score by $-0.0004$ (Section~\ref{sec:budget}), the gap is 9.2 standard deviations wide across three seeds (Section~\ref{sec:seeds}), and five other ways of combining window scores did not close it (Section~\ref{sec:pooling}). Part of the gap comes from the unchosen 0.5 threshold (Section~\ref{sec:threshold}), but even the best threshold leaves the transformer behind.
  \item For finding the clause text, a bag-of-words model is no help because it cannot point to positions in the text, so the transformer is needed here. The small model does well when given the right window: token-F1 0.764, 95\% CI $[0.743, 0.782]$.
  \item For summaries, a set of 41 template sentences turns the task into classification. The fine-tuned model copies a template word for word for 100\% of test clauses, and against clause-specific references it scores no better than the template alone (Section~\ref{sec:summ}). A high automatic score should be checked by reading the outputs.
\end{enumerate}

We withdrew one claim. The AND-ensemble's first place in Table~\ref{tab:ensemble} is not proven. Its lead over the baseline is $+0.0042$. Seed noise alone (SD $0.0015$) would not wipe it out, but the bootstrap over contracts puts it inside $[-0.0037, +0.0115]$ (Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap}), and a different split moves the baseline by $0.0063$ (Section~\ref{sec:splitvar}). Two of the three checks therefore say the top two rows of that table are a tie. The ensemble does earn balanced mistakes and about six times less seed variance than the transformer alone.

Two costs only showed up once we looked for them, and neither is flattering.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item The AND rule does not help everywhere. On the ten rarest categories it gains nothing in F1 over the transformer alone (0.364 against 0.368): it buys 34.6 points of precision with 30.0 points of recall and cuts the clauses found from 39 of 70 to 18 of 70 (Section~\ref{sec:raretail}). All of its overall benefit comes from the common categories, and one of the categories it scores zero on is High-risk.
  \item The separate scores do not multiply. Only 22.0\% of real clauses get through the whole system, against about 65\% predicted by multiplying the separate scores (Section~\ref{sec:e2e}). The presence model's highest-scoring window is the wrong window 30.9\% of the time, and the span model was trained on centred windows it never sees in real use.
\end{itemize}

What links these results is method. Each number in this chapter that we first trusted least (the ensemble's rank, the summarizer's ROUGE, the span model's token-F1) turned out to measure something narrower than it seemed. Each time, we found out by measuring the thing the score stood for: we resampled the contracts, changed the seed, changed the reference sentences, or ran the steps in a chain. None of these checks needed a bigger model or more data.

\subsection{Honest Limitations of the Demo}
\label{sec:demolimits}
By default the app finds clauses with the transformer presence model alone, and the AND-ensemble is offered as a setting. Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap} showed that the transformer alone reports too many clauses, but two measured facts explain why it is still the default.

The first concerns the confidence score, which is not a probability. Section~\ref{sec:threshold} shows that the transformer's score has an Expected Calibration Error of 0.205 and is too confident everywhere. Among detections that score above 0.9, an almost full confidence bar, the clause is really there about 73\% of the time, and among those between 0.5 and 0.6 it is there under 20\% of the time. The app therefore uses the fixed 0.5 threshold of every reported test (an earlier version had a slider for it, which invited reading the score as a probability) and labels every bar as a model score. Fitting a calibration map on a validation split would fix the problem without retraining.

The second is that the transformer alone is the better choice for High-risk clauses. Since the project reports the AND-ensemble as its main setup, a reader might take the app's default as a compromise. Section~\ref{sec:riskeval} shows otherwise where it matters most: the ensemble misses 36.9\% of High-risk clauses and the transformer alone misses 15.9\%. The app's setting is worse on overall micro-F1 and better at finding the clauses a signer would most regret missing. It started as a packaging accident, before we had run the risk-weighted test, and it is now the chosen default. The ensemble is still an option, with one more warning measured on the app itself. On short contracts its TF--IDF half, trained on long SEC filings, blocks many detections. On the 7{,}033-character made-up test contract of Section~\ref{sec:clausify}, the AND rule reports 10 clause types and no High-risk clause, while the transformer alone reports 30 types, 7 of them High-risk.

Long contracts are cut off. To keep the app fast, it scores at most 30 windows, so any contract longer than 45{,}500 characters is only partly read and clauses after that point cannot be found. The app says so in its notice, but none of the numbers in this chapter include the cut, because they score whole documents. It affects many contracts: 38.4\% of CUAD contracts (196 of 510) are longer than 45{,}500 characters, and the app reads only the first 16\% of the longest test contract (289{,}615 characters). Table~\ref{tab:appbench} shows the effect: the 75th-percentile contract and the longest contract take the same time although one is four times longer, because both are cut to 30 windows. The quoted text is also less reliable than the label. Section~\ref{sec:e2e} shows that when the presence model chooses the window, the span model's answer overlaps the real clause in only about a fifth of cases overall. The app now quotes the whole paragraph that the presence model scores highest, which fixed the clear failures we saw in our own tests, though this has not been measured on the test set. For a user, the risk badge and the category name are much more reliable than the quoted text under them, and the app says this too. A card means ``this contract seems to contain a clause of this type; look here'' and should not be read as ``this is the clause''. The risk levels are also set per category. The risk table says a \emph{Cap on Liability} clause needs attention, but it does not check whether a particular cap is generous or harsh. The notice above every analysis states all of this. The demonstration does show that the models work on contracts they have never seen, and that the output can be presented in a form a non-lawyer can act on.
