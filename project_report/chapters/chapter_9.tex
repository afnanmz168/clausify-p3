\section{Summary of Findings}
We set out to turn CUAD's clause-finding benchmark into something a person signing a contract could use: a system that finds the clauses, says how risky they are, and explains them in plain English. On a strict contract-level 80/20 split, the final presence ensemble reaches a micro-F1 of 0.809 and 88.43\% accuracy for finding which clauses are present, and a recall-first setting finds 90.3\% of High-risk clauses. The retrained span model extracts the clause text with a token-F1 of 0.779 on any window that contains it, and 59.6\% of real clauses get through the whole system. The system runs live in the \emph{Clausify} app.

Answering our five research questions (Section~\ref{sec:rqs}) gave two results that changed during the project and one negative one:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=3pt]
  \item The window-based transformer first appeared to lose to the simple word model (RQ1), by 0.082 micro-F1, and no change of training data, seed, pooling rule or threshold closed the gap. All of those runs shared one flaw: the model read only about half of each window, and 23.2\% of its positive training examples were labelled for text it never saw. Reading the whole window and tuning both models on the same validation split, the transformer ties the tuned baseline (0.797 against 0.785), the ensemble beats it (0.809, interval of the lead $[+0.011, +0.037]$), and recall-first settings find 86.9\% to 90.3\% of High-risk clauses, against 63.1\% for the first setup's headline ensemble (RQ1 and RQ5).
  \item The summarizer does not summarize (RQ3). It outputs a category template word for word for every input, and a fixed lookup table matches its score against clause-specific references. We describe it everywhere as a proof-of-concept ``find the category, show its template'' component.
  \item The system first lost most of what its parts found (RQ4). Only 22.1\% of real clauses got through all three steps with the app's setting (22.0\% with the first setup), against about 77\% predicted from the separate scores. Both causes were in our setup. Reading the whole window cut wrong-window choices from 30.9\% to 17.4\%, and retraining the span model on the chunks it really reads, instead of windows centred on the answer, raised extraction in the right window from 28.0\% to 76.4\%. Now 59.6\% of real clauses get through, and what is left is split about evenly between the two causes. Neither fix needed a larger model.
\end{itemize}

RQ5 gave the result we least expected, and we think it is the most useful one for anyone building a system like this. Our evaluation score and our stated goal did not match. In the first setup the AND-ensemble wins on micro-F1 but misses 36.9\% of High-risk clauses, while the OR rule, which comes last on micro-F1, misses only 14.2\%. After the re-run the same conflict appears (the setup with the best micro-F1 finds only 54.0\% of High-risk clauses), and this time we resolved it properly, by fixing a recall-first goal in advance and choosing the setup on a validation split. Half of the clauses behind the overall score are Low-risk ones such as dates, party names and governing law, so a score like micro-F1 should not choose a system built to warn a signer.

Five lessons seem likely to apply beyond this project.

First, a strong simple baseline deserves to be taken seriously, and so does the setup of the model it is compared with. We tested one explanation after another for the transformer's loss and missed the one that mattered, because every run kept the same 256-token cut. A comparison is fair only when both sides read the same evidence and get the same chance to be tuned.

Second, a lead should be compared with its own noise before it is reported. We measured three sources: the bootstrap over contracts (half-width about 0.008), the model seed (SD 0.0015) and the train/test split (SD 0.0063). Two of the three are larger than the $+0.0042$ lead we first used to rank a table. The re-run ensemble's lead of $+0.024$ clears the bootstrap and is 3.8 times the split SD.

Third, an automatic score measures its reference sentences as much as the task. ROUGE-L 0.775 became 0.116 when only the reference sentences changed.

Fourth, test the whole system as well as its parts, and test it against the harm it is meant to prevent. An average that treats a governing-law clause and an unlimited indemnity as equally important hides that harm.

Fifth, measure a limit before relying on it. A one-minute retrieval check (64\%) ruled out a design before it cost any training time. The same habit, applied to sparse attention, corrected us: a Longformer training step does fit in this laptop's memory at batch size 1, which is the opposite of what we had first claimed.

This project delivers a working prototype, a presence ensemble that beats a tuned baseline, and a detailed account of where the system fails. One gap keeps it a prototype: no user has ever been watched using the app, so its main claim, that a report sorted by risk helps a non-lawyer, is untested. Closing it needs people, not more computing power.

\section{Contributions to the Field}
\label{sec:contributions}
The main contributions of this work are:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item A first risk table for the CUAD categories. Each of the 41 categories gets a High\slash Medium\slash Low level and a written reason, judged from the weaker party's side (8 High, 22 Medium, 11 Low), with clear rules for how the levels were given (Section~\ref{sec:riskcriteria}). It gives risk to a category and not to the wording of one clause, which is why we call it category-level risk sorting throughout.
  \item A controlled comparison for finding clauses in long documents. A TF--IDF baseline that reads the whole document (micro-F1 0.775) first looked unbeatable: the window-based DistilBERT lost by 0.082, and more data, other seeds, other pooling rules and other thresholds did not close the gap. The cause was in our setup, since the transformer read only about half of each window. With the whole window read and both models tuned on the same validation split, they tie (0.797 against 0.785) and their ensemble wins (0.809, interval of the lead $[+0.011, +0.037]$). A control run that changes only the input length shows that the whole window is worth $+0.036$ micro-F1 to the tuned transformer, and that without it tuning alone leaves the transformer behind. Recall-first settings chosen the same way find up to 90.3\% of High-risk clauses.
  \item A measurement of how errors add up. Testing the three steps in a chain showed that only about 22\% of real clauses got through the whole system, against 65--77\% predicted from the separate scores. The test also found the two causes, a wrong choice of window and a mismatch between training and real use, and fixing both (reading the whole window, and retraining the span model on the chunks it really reads) raises the share to 59.6\%. Neither fix needed a larger model.
  \item A demonstration that a small set of template sentences turns summarizing into classification. Testing the same 150 clauses against our 41 category templates and against clause-specific references drops ROUGE-L from 0.775 to 0.116, and the fine-tuned model outputs a template word for word for 100\% of inputs.
  \item A simple safeguard: measure the limit before training. Question-guided retrieval of windows caps the possible recall at 64\%, and that one measurement was enough to reject the retrieval approach before any training time was spent on it.
  \item A system that can be rebuilt on a laptop. All three models train on a single Apple-Silicon laptop. The split, the trained weights and a separate test notebook are saved, so every number in this project can be produced again from disk.
  \item Clausify, a prototype web app that runs the trained models live and shows the results grouped by risk. It demonstrates the system but has not been tested with users, and Section~\ref{sec:demolimits} records what it does not do well.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
The results in Chapter~5 depend on choices that could change the conclusions if they were wrong. We list them under the four usual headings and say in each case whether we measured the threat or only note it.

\subsection{Internal Validity: Could Our Own Method Have Produced These Numbers?}
The first setup tuned no settings (Section~\ref{sec:valprotocol}). That removed the usual threat, because no setting was chosen on the test set, but it created the opposite one: the transformer competed with default settings, and with half of each window cut off, against a baseline that has almost no settings to get wrong. This turned out to be the most serious threat in the project, and the re-run in Section~\ref{sec:v2} answers it by tuning both models in the same way on a validation split.

The re-run trained on 327 contracts instead of 408 and chose the transformer's epoch on validation. A control run with the original 256-token input under the same protocol shows that these changes have no measurable effect (0.693 against the original 0.692 without tuning), so the gain we report comes from reading the whole window (Section~\ref{sec:v2}). One threat remains. The re-run made many choices on the same 81 validation contracts, including up to 41 thresholds per model. With so few contracts some per-category thresholds will fit noise. That makes the validation scores optimistic (0.857 for the tuned ensemble, against 0.809 on the test set) but leaves the test scores fair, because the test set played no part in any choice.

An early test run of the summarizer tracked its loss on test-split clauses (Section~\ref{sec:valprotocol}). No model was chosen from that run, and the final model was trained again without any evaluation set, so no reported score depends on it. It was still the wrong practice, and the ``validation'' loss it printed should be ignored. Figure~\ref{fig:sumloss} now shows the October 2026 re-training, which used no evaluation set.

Some design choices were made after looking at the training data. This is allowed, but the line needs care. The window size and step follow from the median clause length measured on training contracts. The 64\% retrieval limit, however, was measured on the whole dataset, test contracts included. That measurement ruled out a design and did not choose a setting, and it was made before any model existed, so we judge the risk of leakage to be negligible. It is not zero, and if we did the work again we would make the split first.

The span model and the summarizer have no seed variation. Only the presence model was re-run with new seeds (Section~\ref{sec:seeds}). The first span model and the summarizer were each trained again with the same seed and gave the same scores to within 0.001, and the retrained span model was trained once, so their seed variance is still unmeasured.

\subsection{Construct Validity: Do Our Scores Measure What We Claim?}
ROUGE-L does not measure summary quality here, and we can show it. Against our 41 templates it gives 0.775. Against clause-specific references it gives 0.116, and a fixed lookup table scores almost the same, 0.115 (Section~\ref{sec:summ}). We report both numbers because the first one alone would give a false picture. We still have no measure of faithfulness or readability (Section~\ref{sec:lit_faith}), so even 0.116 does not show that the output keeps the legal meaning.

Micro-F1 does not measure harm to the user, and Section~\ref{sec:riskeval} shows the two can point in opposite directions. The setup that wins on micro-F1 misses 36.9\% of High-risk clauses, while a setup that comes last misses 14.2\%. Half of the clauses behind the overall score are Low-risk ones such as dates, party names and governing law. Any claim like ``this system is 77.9\% good'' has this problem.

Category-level risk is not clause-level risk (Section~\ref{sec:risk}). Our levels say that a category usually deserves attention. They say nothing about whether a particular cap is generous or harsh, so calling the output a risk assessment would claim too much, and we call it sorting by risk.

The risk levels are our own. The risk-weighted results use our choice of which categories are High, Medium and Low (Section~\ref{sec:riskcriteria}), and a different choice would change those numbers.

Token-F1 against one marked span assumes that the clause has clear edges. Section~\ref{sec:span} notes that the edges of legal clauses are often debatable, and Section~\ref{sec:errorexamples} shows at least one ``false negative'' that looks like a disagreement with the labels.

\subsection{External Validity: Where Do These Results Apply?}
Every number comes from CUAD, which holds 510 US business contracts taken from SEC filings, in English. We have not tested a single contract from outside that dataset. We therefore have no evidence about other legal traditions or countries, including Bangladeshi contracts, which are the setting that motivated this work. The same goes for consumer and employment contracts, which are built differently and have a different weaker party, for contracts not in English, for contracts much shorter or longer than CUAD's, and for recent machine-written contracts. The risk table has one more threat of this kind. Whether a non-compete can be enforced depends on the country or state, so a level that is right in one place may be wrong in another. Because CUAD comes from SEC filings, it also leans towards large-company contracts where both sides have lawyers, which is close to the opposite of the small businesses and individuals the system is meant for.

\subsection{Statistical Conclusion Validity: Can the Intervals Be Trusted?}
We measured three sources of variation, and they differ in size. The bootstrap over contracts gives an interval half-width of about 0.008 (Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap}), the ensemble's model-seed SD is 0.0015 over three runs (Section~\ref{sec:seeds}), and the split SD is 0.0063 over twenty splits (Section~\ref{sec:splitvar}). The bootstrap and the split variation are both larger than the ensemble's $+0.0042$ lead, which is why we call it a tie. The split variation is the largest, and we could measure it only for the baseline.

Three seeds is a small sample for estimating a standard deviation, so the uncertainty around 0.0015 is itself large.

Scores for single rare categories are not meaningful. Five categories have at most seven test examples. We report their F1 values for completeness, and no claim in this project depends on any one of them.

We used one split and one dataset. The bootstrap resamples contracts within our test set. It cannot show how results would change on another dataset, and no interval in this project covers that.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
Section~\ref{sec:threats} covered the threats to each kind of validity. This section lists the limits of the system itself, meaning what it does not do. Each limit is described where it comes up in the text, so these are pointers.

There has been no usability study (Section~\ref{sec:usability}), and nobody outside the three of us has used Clausify. The project argues that a report sorted by risk is more useful to a non-lawyer than a plain list of clauses, without evidence: there is no measure of task completion, understanding, trust or over-reliance. Section~\ref{sec:threshold} shows that the confidence bar is too confident, and Section~\ref{sec:summ} shows that the plain-English sentence is the same for every clause of a category, so over-reliance is what a usability study would need to look for.

We used small models (DistilBERT, FLAN-T5-small) to fit a laptop's computing budget, so our scores are lower than the same method would reach with larger models on CUDA hardware.

Training used one seed and one data budget, which we checked only in part. Section~\ref{sec:budget} reports what happened when the presence transformer was trained on all 53{,}662 window examples instead of a sample of 24{,}000, and Section~\ref{sec:seeds} reports the spread across three training seeds. The first span model and the summarizer were each trained twice with the same seed (matching to within 0.001), never with a new seed, and on a sample of the data (8{,}000 of 11{,}156 and 4{,}000 of 11{,}156); the retrained span model was trained once, on 6{,}000 clauses. Their seed variance is unmeasured, and their scores should be read with that in mind.

The rarest categories are close to impossible to learn with this much data, and Section~\ref{sec:raretail} shows that the AND-ensemble makes this worse. On the ten rarest categories the AND rule gains no F1 over the transformer alone, buying precision only with recall, and it scores zero on three categories that the transformer alone was partly finding. One of the rare categories is High-risk in our table, so this limits the real system as well as the ranking.

The span test covers less than the CUAD paper's test. Section~\ref{sec:span} measures extraction inside windows that contain the answer, and we did not run the heavier full-document precision-at-recall test. Section~\ref{sec:e2e} closes part of that gap by measuring what gets through the real system, but it uses a different method, and we do not present the two as comparable.

The summarizer does not summarize. Section~\ref{sec:summ} shows this with numbers: the fine-tuned model outputs one of 41 pre-written templates word for word for every input, and against clause-specific references it scores no better than the template alone with no model at all. It is a ``find the category, show its template'' component and should be described as one.

The risk table is set per category: it flags how risky a category usually is, and it does not judge how harsh the exact wording in front of the user is.

Everything rests on one dataset from one country. Every number in this project comes from CUAD's 510 US business contracts taken from SEC filings. We have not tested the system on a single contract from outside that dataset, so we have no evidence about how it handles other ways of writing contracts, and none at all about the Bangladeshi setting that motivated the work.

\section{Recommendations for Future Work}
\label{sec:futurework}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item Watch people use Clausify. Five to ten people would each review a contract with and without the app, and we would measure the time taken, the clauses correctly found and, most of all, whether users trust the output too much. The project claims that a report sorted by risk helps a non-lawyer, and nothing in it tests that claim yet.
  \item Compare with a large language model. Run a prompted LLM on the same 102 test contracts with the same scores, either an open legal model run locally (such as SaulLM-7B \cite{colombo2024saullm}) or a hosted one, and count how often it reports clauses that are not there (Section~\ref{sec:lit_llm}). It is the comparison a reader is most likely to ask for, and we did not run it.
  \item Train a model to choose the window. With the retrained span model, the window the presence model chooses is one of the two remaining losses: it misses the clause for 17.4\% of detected categories, 206 clauses against the 244 the span model misses in the right window (Section~\ref{sec:spanv2}). A model trained to locate the clause, instead of reusing the presence score, would target it, and the span model, which can now say ``not here'', could score several candidate windows.
  \item Train the span model further. The retrained model used 6{,}000 clauses, one seed and two epochs, and still misses 23.6\% of clauses in the right window. Training on all the clauses, and measuring the spread across seeds, are the obvious next steps.
  \item Test summaries for faithfulness. Word overlap is not enough; BERTScore, a check that the summary follows from the clause, a readability score and a small human rating of whether the legal meaning is kept would all help (Section~\ref{sec:lit_faith}).
  \item Learn how to pool windows. The test in Section~\ref{sec:pooling} tried only fixed rules for combining window scores. A learned rule that weighs windows by relevance (attention pooling) is the untested option most likely to help.
  \item Use cross-validation. The split is the largest uncertainty we measured (Section~\ref{sec:splitvar}), and we could only measure it for the baseline.
  \item Build a summarizer that really summarizes. Grow the 41 templates into a set of targets that differ from clause to clause, and fine-tune again. The 150-clause trial set in Section~\ref{sec:summ} is a working first version of both the labelling method and the test that would show success, and scaling it to a few thousand clauses is the way from picking templates to simplifying clauses.
  \item Tune for risk directly. The re-run chose its recall-first settings with micro-F2, which treats every category alike. A goal that weights High-risk categories more, chosen on validation in the same way, would aim the thresholds at the clauses that matter most. The mixed rule suggested in Section~\ref{sec:raretail} was among the candidates in the re-run, and the validation split did not choose it.
  \item Try meaning-based retrieval. Our 64\% retrieval limit (Section~\ref{sec:ceiling}) was measured only with TF--IDF matching. A sentence-encoder retriever matches on meaning and might raise that limit enough to make a ``retrieve, then read'' design competitive. The measurement is cheap, and we should have run it.
  \item Train a sparse-attention baseline. Section~\ref{sec:lit_long} argues against Longformer partly from arithmetic and partly from cost, and Section~\ref{sec:lfbench} measures the cost, but nobody has trained one on this task. On CUDA hardware a Longformer baseline would be the fair comparison that our argument currently replaces with reasoning.
  \item Use larger models. Re-running the same system with DeBERTa-v3-base or large and FLAN-T5-base on CUDA hardware would close the gap to the published CUAD results.
  \item Run the full-document span test. Building the ranked whole-document test would let us report precision at 80\% recall and compare directly with the CUAD paper.
  \item Find conflicts between clauses. The rule engine designed in an earlier phase of the project (for example, \emph{Exclusivity} against \emph{Most Favored Nation}) could be brought into the app.
  \item Give risk to each clause. Moving from risk per category to risk per clause means reading the extracted text itself, such as the amount of a cap or the length of a non-compete.
  \item Test contracts from outside CUAD. Even three local English-language business contracts, run through the system and checked by hand, would say more about how it generalizes than any further tuning on CUAD, and they are the first step towards the Bangladeshi use this work was aimed at.
\end{itemize}
